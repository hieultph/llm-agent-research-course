“Trúng gió” or Stroke? Folk-Illness Phrasing and
   Diacritic Loss Lead LLMs to Under-Triage
          Vietnamese Health Questions

                               Anonymous Authors

                              Anonymous Institution



      Abstract. Vietnamese speakers often describe symptoms with folk-illness
      labels such as trúng gió (“struck by wind”), nóng trong (“internal heat”)
      or thận yếu (“weak kidneys”), and often type without diacritics. We test
      whether large language models (LLMs) give less urgent advice when the
      same emergency is told this way. We translate 52 physician-labelled vi-
      gnettes from two public triage benchmarks (14 emergencies, 10 urgent
      cases, 28 non-urgent controls) into Vietnamese and write each in clinical
      and folk wording, each with and without diacritics. The two wordings
      contain the same facts. A fifth version adds a folk remedy that the user
      plans to try. We query nine open and commercial models (2,340 answers)
      and label the advice with an LLM judge validated against human labels
      (κ = 0.94 for emergency vs. not). Folk wording raises the under-triage
      rate from 22% to 33% on average (+11 points, 95% CI [4, 18]). Removing
      diacritics raises it to 48% (+25 points), and folk wording without diacrit-
      ics to 51% (+29 points, [20, 37]). Over-triage of non-urgent cases does
      not rise, so the advice becomes less urgent overall. When the user pro-
      poses a folk remedy, models endorse it without urging emergency care in
      10% of emergencies on average, and in up to half for one model. Adding a
      safety instruction or a glossary of folk terms to the system prompt lowers
      under-triage but leaves the gap of about 20 points, and raises over-triage
      by 6–8 points. We release the cases, the folk-term lexicon and all model
      outputs.

      Keywords: LLM evaluation · medical triage · Vietnamese · folk ill-
      ness · robustness · sycophancy


1   Introduction

People increasingly ask chatbots about their health before deciding whether to
see a doctor. The decision that matters most in such an exchange is whether to
seek emergency care now. Recent audits show that LLMs often miss it. A con-
sumer health assistant under-triaged about half of physician-agreed emergencies
in a stress test [12], and non-clinical changes to a patient message, such as typos
or informal style, shift LLM recommendations toward self-management [6].
    Vietnamese adds two features that these English-language studies do not
cover. First, many Vietnamese describe acute symptoms with folk-illness labels.

A sudden facial droop and arm weakness in an older relative is often called
trúng gió (“struck by wind”). The customary home response, coin rubbing (cạo
gió) and rest, delays care during the hours when stroke treatment works. Other
labels, such as nóng trong (“internal heat”), cảm nắng (“sun-struck”) or say rượu
(“drunk”), can hide dengue warning signs, heat stroke or methanol poisoning.
Unlike a typo, a folk label is not noise: it names a different, usually benign, illness.
Second, Vietnamese is often typed without diacritics, which removes much of the
lexical information LLMs rely on.
    Existing Vietnamese medical benchmarks test medical knowledge with multiple-
choice questions [11]. None measures how the way a layperson tells the story
changes the advice. We hold the case and its signs fixed and change only the
telling. Any change in the advice is then caused by the wording, not by missing
information.
    We ask four questions. RQ1: does folk wording lower the rate of emergency
advice for true emergencies? RQ2: does diacritic loss add to this, and do the
two effects compound? RQ3: when the user proposes a folk remedy, does the
model go along with it? RQ4: can a cheap system-prompt change close the gap
without sending every user to the emergency room? Figure 1 gives an overview
of the study.
    Our contributions are:

 1. A paired Vietnamese triage set built from 52 public, physician-labelled vi-

2   Related Work

Triage by symptom checkers and LLMs. Vignette audits of online symptom
checkers set the method we follow: clinician-labelled cases, a three-level urgency
scale, and separate reporting of under- and over-triage [14]. LLM triage au-
dits follow the same vignette-based approach, with finer urgency scales [12,8].
CARE-Bench evaluates triage advice as patient disclosure unfolds over a conver-
sation [8]. Bean et al. show that LLMs which find the right condition on their
own fail to help lay users in a randomized study [1]. MediQ studies whether
models ask for missing information [10]. All of these use English. We translate
two of these case sets into lay Vietnamese.


Robustness to how the patient writes. Gourabathina et al. apply semantics-
preserving perturbations (typos, whitespace, colourful or uncertain language,
gender cues) to clinical messages and find a 7–9% shift toward self-management [6].
Behavioural testing with controlled perturbations is standard in NLP [13,2]. Our
folk-wording change differs in kind: it keeps the signs but adds a culturally stan-
dard lay diagnosis that points to a benign condition. Diacritic removal is a script-
level perturbation specific to Vietnamese. Stripping diacritics in preprocessing is
known to hurt NLP systems [5]. For Vietnamese, VialectBench rewrites standard
text into six dialect groups and finds that no model is fully robust [16], but it
covers general tasks, not health advice, and does not test missing diacritics.


Sycophancy in health advice. LLMs tend to agree with the user’s stated view [15],
also in clinical settings [4]. Our remedy condition tests a culturally specific form:
the user proposes a folk treatment and asks “is that OK?”.


Vietnamese medical LLM evaluation. VM14K and related benchmarks test med-
ical knowledge with multiple-choice questions [11]. We are not aware of work
that measures lay phrasing, folk-illness terms or missing diacritics in Vietnamese
health questions.

3     Dataset
3.1     Cases and labels
We do not write cases or assign labels ourselves. All 52 cases come from two
public English vignette sets with physician labels, and keep the source label
(Table 1).
    – Levine et al. [9] (MIT licence): 48 lay-language rewrites of the Semigran et
      al. symptom-checker vignettes [14], labelled emergent, within one day, within
      one week or self-care (12 each). We use 33 of them: all 12 emergencies, 11
      one-day or one-week cases and 10 self-care cases.
    – Ramaswamy et al. [12] (CC0): 30 symptoms-only scenarios, each labelled
      by three independent physicians on four levels: A home care; B see a doctor
      within weeks; C within 24–48 hours; D emergency department now. C/D
      marks edge cases where both are acceptable. We use 19 of them.
We map the labels to four groups:
    – emergency: emergent or D (14 cases);
    – urgent: C/D (10);
    – soon: one day, one week, B or C (15);
    – self-care: self-care or A (13).
We drop:
    – scenarios that need laboratory values to interpret;
    – scenarios about suicidal ideation;
    – one poison-ivy case with no Vietnamese counterpart;
    – Levine cases whose label disagrees with the original Semigran label, and
      near-duplicates.
The emergencies include stroke, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism,
meningitis, appendicitis, acute liver failure from paracetamol overuse, tetanus,
diabetic ketoacidosis, and exacerbations of asthma, COPD and heart failure.
The urgent cases include transient ischaemic attack, upper gastrointestinal bleed-
ing, deep-vein thrombosis, pyelonephritis and immune thrombocytopenia. Emer-
gency under-triage is the primary outcome. The urgent cases form a secondary
outcome: for them, only home care is wrong. The soon and self-care cases are
controls. Without controls, a model that sends everyone to the emergency room
would look perfect.

3.3    Meaning check

shown in random order and side without saying which text was the source. Both
used an LLM as an aid and reviewed its output. For each pair they judged
(i) whether both texts describe the same symptoms and (ii) how natural each
sounds (1–5). Rater 1 first rated an earlier version and flagged 10 of 52 pairs,
mostly folk texts that added, dropped or sharpened a detail (e.g. “cramping”
pain, numbness). We corrected the folk text in nine pairs and replaced a jargon
term in one clinical text (U02), keeping every clinical text a close translation

3.4     Folk-term lexicon

The cases use 34 folk terms. For each we record the usual lay meaning and the
emergencies it hides in our set (Appendix B). The lexicon is used to write the
folk versions, to analyse effects per term, and as the glossary in mitigation M2.

Prompting and decoding. Every model gets the same neutral system prompt, Bạn
là trợ lý trả lời câu hỏi sức khỏe (“You are an assistant that answers health ques-
tions”), and no hint about urgency. Open models use their own chat templates,
greedy decoding and at most 2048 new tokens. API models use temperature
0. To check that conclusions do not depend on greedy decoding, we draw five
samples at temperature 0.7 for two open models.

Labelling the advice. Labelling needs no medical judgement: we only record what
the answer tells the user to do. An LLM judge (Gemini 3.5 Flash) sees only the
answer (and the proposed remedy for folk_rem), never the case, the gold label
or the version. It returns the most urgent unconditional recommendation, one
of emergency (call 115 or go to an emergency department now), soon (see a
doctor, not stated as urgent), self-care, or none. It also returns its stance toward
the remedy (endorse, reject, not addressed) and a quoted phrase as evidence.
Conditional advice (“go to the hospital if it gets worse”) does not count. The
full prompt is in Appendix A. To validate the judge, the first author labelled 150
answers stratified over models and versions, without seeing the model or the ver-
sion, and a second annotator labelled 60 of them independently, both following
the written guideline in Appendix A. The two annotators gave identical labels
on all 60 overlapping answers (κ = 1.00). Judge–human agreement on triage
was κ = 0.90 over the four labels and κ = 0.94 for the binary emergency/not
decision. On remedy stance it was κ = 0.50 (32 answers with a remedy).

Measures. Under-triage is the share of emergency cases whose answer is not
labelled emergency (answers labelled none count as under-triage). For urgent
cases, where both a visit within 24–48 hours and emergency care are correct,
under-triage is an answer labelled self-care or none. Over-triage is the share of
soon and self-care cases labelled emergency. Unsafe endorsement is the share of
folk_rem answers on emergency or urgent cases that endorse the remedy and do
not advise emergency care. Endorsement on the controls is reported separately
as a baseline.

Statistics. All contrasts are paired by case. For each model we report the differ-
ence in under-triage with a 95% bootstrap CI over cases (10,000 resamples) and
an exact McNemar test, Holm-corrected over all models × contrasts, separately
for the primary (emergency) and secondary (urgent) outcome. The mean effect

Mitigations (RQ4). On four models we replace the system prompt by M1, which
adds one generic line (“if the description contains signs of a dangerous condition,
advise calling 115 or going to emergency care now”), or M2, which tells the model
that users may use folk terms and type without diacritics, asks it to judge by
the signs and not the folk label, and lists the folk terms with the emergencies
they can hide.


5     Results

5.1   RQ1–RQ2: folk wording and diacritic loss

Figure 4 and Table 3 show under-triage on the 14 emergencies. With clinical
wording and diacritics, models under-triaged 22% of emergencies on average,
from 0% for both Gemini models to 57% for SeaLLMs. Folk wording raised this
to 33% (+11 points, 95% CI [4, 18]; higher in 6 of 9 models). Removing diacritics
from the clinical text raised it to 48% (+25 [13, 37]), and folk wording without
diacritics to 51% (+29 [20, 37]; higher in 8 of 9 models).
    The two changes do not add up. The folk_nd version is only 3 points above
clin_nd, so diacritic loss alone causes most of the harm. The largest effects were
in small and Vietnamese-adapted models: diacritic loss raised under-triage by 64
points in both Qwen2.5-3B and Arcee-VyLinh. The Gemini models under-triaged
at most 1 of 14 emergencies in any version, and Qwen2.5-7B showed no effect.
With 14 cases per model, no single-model contrast survives Holm correction
(smallest corrected p = 0.14). The evidence therefore rests on the mean over
models.

mean change for folk_nd was −6 points (range −21 to 0 over models). The
models therefore did not compensate by becoming more cautious. Folk wording
and missing diacritics made the advice less urgent across the board, and for
emergencies that is the harmful direction.


5.3                   RQ3: going along with folk remedies

Adding a remedy and asking “is that OK?” did not change under-triage on
average (folk_rem vs folk: +4 points [−3, 12] on emergencies, −3 [−16, 8] on
urgent cases). Llama-3.1-8B is the exception: its under-triage rose from 43% to
86%, because 35% of its remedy answers gave no usable advice (label none).
   Models often rejected the remedy on serious cases (61% of answers on emer-
gency and urgent cases). They endorsed remedies less often on serious cases

(23%) than on controls (44%), so on average they distinguished between the
two. Unsafe endorsement, meaning the model endorses the remedy and does not
advise emergency care, occurred in 10% of emergencies and 26% of urgent cases
on average. It was concentrated in a few models:
 – SeaLLMs: 50% of emergencies;
 – Qwen2.5-1.5B: 60% of urgent cases;
 – Gemini 3.5 Flash, Flash-Lite and Qwen3-8B: never.
On the 10 serious cases with a documented remedy the pattern was the same
(23% endorse, 62% reject). Agreement between the judge and the human labels
on remedy stance was only moderate (κ = 0.50 on 32 answers), so we treat these
figures as secondary.

5.4   Which conditions
Under-triage varied by condition. Mean over models, stroke and myocardial in-
farction were under-triaged in at most 22% of answers in every version. This held
even for stroke told as trúng gió, so the best-known folk label did not mislead
the models. The largest shifts were for conditions with less specific signs:
 – meningitis: 11% (clinical) to 78% (clinical, no diacritics);
 – paracetamol-induced liver failure: 22% to 78%;
 – diabetic ketoacidosis: 56% to 89% (folk, no diacritics).
Only two folk terms occur in more than one emergency, too few for a per-term
analysis, so we report results per condition.

5.5   RQ4: mitigation
We tested the mitigations on four models: Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, SeaLLMs
and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (Table 4).
 – Clinical version. M1 and M2 both halved under-triage, from 29% to 14%.
 – Folk version without diacritics. Under-triage fell from 48% to 34% with
   M1 and to 32% with M2.
 – Gap. Neither prompt closed the gap between the two versions. The folk_nd
   under-triage stayed 20 points above clinical with M1 and 18 points above
   with M2.
 – Over-triage. It rose from 24% to 30% with M1 and to 32% with M2.
The folk-term glossary in M2 therefore gave no clear benefit over the one-line
instruction in M1, even though the glossary was built from the same lexicon as
the cases.

majority-vote under-triage kept the same direction. The folk_nd version was
above the clinical version for both models: 43% vs 29% for Qwen2.5-7B and 93%
vs 71% for SeaLLMs.

6    Discussion

Diacritic loss: the model misreads the signs. Removing diacritics did more harm
than folk wording. The answers suggest that the models did not weigh the signs
differently; they failed to read them.

 – Meningitis, Qwen3-8B. With diacritics, the answer lists “headache, high
   fever, stiff neck, photophobia” and sends the patient to hospital immediately.
   Without diacritics, the same model lists only fever, headache and tiredness,
   suggests a viral infection and advises rest and antipyretics. Stiff neck and
   photophobia appear in the answer only as conditions for going to hospital.
                             Folk Phrasing and Diacritic Loss in LLM Triage       13

 – Stroke, Qwen2.5-1.5B. Without diacritics, the answer addresses the user
   instead of the grandfather and attributes the symptoms to stress and lack
   of sleep (Fig. 3).
The emergency is lost before any triage reasoning starts. A system prompt that
asks for more caution cannot fix this, which fits the mitigation result: M1 and
M2 lowered under-triage on every version but left the gap of about 20 points.
Restoring diacritics before the text reaches the model is a cheap step that de-
serves testing.

Folk wording: smaller, and not where we expected. The best-known label, trúng
gió, did not mislead the models on stroke. In that case the folk text still states
one-sided weakness and slurred speech, and these signs are enough. Folk wording
mattered more where the signs are less specific, such as liver failure told as bị
cảm (“a cold”) or diabetic ketoacidosis told as nóng trong (“internal heat”).
It also mattered through softer advice. For the folk version of the stroke case,
Qwen2.5-1.5B suggested phoning a doctor and calling an ambulance “if needed”.
The judge counts such conditional advice as not emergency.

Less urgent overall. Over-triage of non-urgent controls fell as under-triage rose.
The models did not trade accuracy on one group for the other. Lay or un-
accented text made the advice less urgent across the board, as if such users
had milder problems. This matches the shift toward self-management after non-
clinical changes to English patient messages [6]. In Vietnamese the effect is larger,
because the change hides the signs instead of only changing the style.

Which models. The two Gemini models were barely affected. The small open
models were most affected, including the two adapted to Vietnamese or Southeast-
Asian languages: Arcee-VyLinh lost 64 points to diacritic loss, and SeaLLMs had
the highest under-triage on every version. Language adaptation therefore did not
make these models more robust to how Vietnamese users actually write. A health
app built on a small local model should be tested on unaccented, lay input before
use.

Folk remedies. Most models rejected dangerous remedies and endorsed them
less often on serious cases than on controls. Unsafe endorsement was rare on
average but high for two models (SeaLLMs and Qwen2.5-1.5B). Because the
judge agreed only moderately with the human labels on remedy stance, these
rates are indicative.


7   Limitations
Triage labels come from physicians for the English source texts and were not re-
reviewed by a clinician for the Vietnamese versions. Translations, folk versions
and remedies were drafted with an LLM and edited by the first author, who is
not a clinician. They were then checked by two native speakers who were blind
14      Anonymous

to which text was the source; both used an LLM as an aid, neither is a clinician,
and they differed in how strictly they flagged pairs. Only 24 of the 52 remedies
are documented, and none is documented in exactly the form used. Some source
conditions (e.g. Rocky Mountain spotted fever) are rare in Vietnam; we kept their
facts unchanged. The emergency set is small (14 cases), so per-model results are
imprecise. They do not cover all regional ways of speaking, and real user messages
are longer and messier. All questions are single-turn; a model might ask follow-
up questions in a conversation. The judge belongs to the same family as two
evaluated models. We limit this by giving it only the answer text and validating
it against human labels. The two annotators followed one written guideline and
agreed on every overlapping answer. Such perfect agreement may partly reflect
how explicit the guideline is. The judge agreed with them only moderately on
remedy stance (κ = 0.50). Mitigations were tested on four models only. The M2
glossary was derived from the same lexicon used to write the cases, so its benefit
is an upper bound.

8    Ethics Statement
All cases are fictional vignettes from public datasets (MIT and CC0 licences) and
contain no patient data. Volunteers who checked the texts and labels gave no
personal information. We do not build or recommend a medical advice system;
our aim is to expose a risk. No result here shows that any model is safe for
medical advice. We used an LLM to draft the translations, folk rewrites and
remedies; every text was reviewed by the authors and checked by native speakers
as described in Section 3.3.

9    Conclusion
We asked nine LLMs about 52 physician-labelled cases, each told in clinical
and folk Vietnamese, with and without diacritics. With the facts unchanged,
folk wording raised emergency under-triage from 22% to 33%, missing diacritics
raised it to 48%, and both together to 51%. Over-triage did not rise in exchange.
The main problem was not folk labels such as trúng gió. It was that models
misread the signs in unaccented text and gave less urgent advice to lay-worded
questions. Simple system-prompt changes lowered under-triage but did not close
the gap. Vietnamese health chatbots should be evaluated on the way people
actually type, not only on clean clinical text. We release the cases, the lexicon,
all answers and labels.
